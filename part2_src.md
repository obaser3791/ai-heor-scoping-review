This is the second of two articles. Part 1 measured the literature on artificial intelligence in health economics and outcomes research and validated its own classification. Part 2 assembles the regulatory, health technology assessment, and industry record, examines what the economics of automation implies for this field, and proposes a reporting standard.

## Short Summary

Pharmaceutical companies report time savings of 40% to 97% from artificial intelligence in regulatory and evidence work. Three of 1,391 health technology assessment documents disclose using it. This second article of a two part series sets the claims against the record: what the Food and Drug Administration and European regulators have written, what agencies have accepted, what industry has published, and how these tools fail. It reads the gap through the productivity arithmetic of Nobel laureate Daron Acemoglu, whose distinction between tasks with observable answers and tasks without one predicts the pattern almost exactly, and it proposes TRACE-HEOR, ten reporting items whose central requirement is a decision reversal analysis.

## Abstract

**BACKGROUND**

Part 1 of this series found that measured gains from artificial intelligence (AI) in health economics and outcomes research (HEOR) concentrate in tasks with observable ground truth and disappear where the quantity of interest is a counterfactual. What regulators, health technology assessment (HTA) bodies, and sponsors have said about that division has not been assembled in one place.

**METHODS**

We assembled the primary regulatory, HTA, industry, and journal policy record through September 2026, requiring for each claim a named primary source and, where a quantity was reported, its provenance and whether the reporting party had a commercial interest. We downloaded the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) AI enabled medical device list in full, tabulated published economic evaluations of AI, and set reported industry efficiency gains against disclosed use in submissions. We then evaluated the field against the task based productivity framework of Nobel laureate Daron Acemoglu and derived a reporting standard from the documented failure modes.

**RESULTS**

The FDA has authorized 1,614 AI enabled devices, 1,230 (76.2%) in radiology, rising from 18 in 2016 to 335 in 2025, and has received more than 500 drug and biologic submissions containing AI components since 2016; its January 2025 credibility assessment guidance remains a draft and excludes discovery and operational uses. Industry reports time savings of 40% to 97% in regulatory writing and approximately 80% in payer dossier drafting, every figure single arm and reported by the deploying or selling party. Disclosed AI use is 3 of 1,391 HTA documents (0.2%) and 0.6% of one journal's submissions, against surveys reporting use between 25% and 81%. Nineteen economic evaluations of AI have been published against 1,614 authorized devices. No regulator or HTA body has been documented accepting or rejecting an AI derived endpoint as such.

**CONCLUSIONS**

Current disclosure instruments ask whether AI wrote the text. Reviewers need to know whether AI selected the evidence, defined the cohort, derived the endpoint, or generated the code, and whether the recommendation would change without it.

## Introduction

Part 1 of this series reported a systematic scoping review of 2,476 indexed studies on artificial intelligence in health economics and outcomes research published between 2010 and 2025, and measured the error rate of its own classification against a blinded independent classifier. Two findings carry forward. First, the size of this literature is not known to within a factor of two: the corpus reduces to approximately 1,271 genuinely HEOR relevant studies (95% confidence interval [CI] 1,131 to 1,410), and the fastest growing label, economic evaluation, is the one that could least reliably be assigned (Cohen kappa 0.144). Second, measured gains concentrate in tasks with an observable answer and are absent where the quantity of interest is a counterfactual{{c:10.1038/s41746-025-01457-w;10.1016/j.jval.2025.08.002;10.1007/s41669-024-00477-8}}.

That division sorts the field into two layers. A clerical layer, comprising screening, extraction, code drafting, and dossier assembly, has measurable ground truth and documented gains. An inferential layer, comprising cohort definition, endpoint derivation, confounder selection, and effect estimation, has no supervised target, because no patient is observed under both the treatment and the comparator. The two layers carry different risks and should be governed differently.

This second article asks what the institutional record says about that distinction. Regulators come first, because a reimbursement dossier is a regulated document and because the FDA has written the most developed framework while becoming one of the largest users of these tools. HTA agencies come second, because they will absorb whatever the automation produces. Industry comes third, because the efficiency claims driving adoption originate there and almost none has been independently replicated. We then turn to how these systems fail, to what productivity economics predicts for a field whose central quantity is unobservable, and to what reporting would make an AI assisted submission auditable.

## Methods

The review methods, search strategy, classification dictionary, and validation procedure are reported in full in Part 1 and archived in the public replication package{{c:github_repo}}. This article draws on the narrative evidence review conducted alongside that scoping review.

For the regulatory, HTA, industry, and journal policy record we searched agency, society, and company sources through September 2026 and required a named primary source for every claim. Where a quantity was reported we recorded its provenance, whether a peer reviewed study, a conference abstract, a regulatory document, or a company communication, and whether the reporting party had a commercial interest in the result. Conference abstracts and vendor communications are labelled rather than excluded, because in this field they carry information available nowhere else.

## Results

### THE REGULATORY RECORD

The FDA's position is the most developed and the least finished. Table 1 summarizes the instruments now in force or in draft.

For drugs and biologics, the January 2025 draft guidance proposes a seven step credibility assessment framework in which a sponsor defines the question of interest, the context of use, and the model risk, then establishes credibility proportionate to that risk{{c:fda_draft_ai_guidance}}. The definition of model risk is the operative sentence: "Model risk is a combination of two factors: (a) model influence... and (b) decision consequence." Two features are routinely missed. The guidance explicitly excludes AI used in drug discovery and in operational efficiencies, so most publicly announced pharmaceutical AI deployments fall outside it. And it remains a draft as of September 2026, with the comment period closed on 7 April 2025. The agency's account of the evidence behind it names an FDA sponsored workshop convened by the Duke Margolis Institute for Health Policy in December 2022, more than 800 comments from 65 entities, and experience with more than 500 submissions containing AI components since 2016{{c:fda_press_ai_framework;10.1056/aipc2500801}}. PhRMA asked the agency to clarify which device provisions apply, how human in the loop mitigation affects the risk assessment, and how models composed of multiple models are treated{{c:phrma_comments}}, a fair summary of what the framework does not yet answer.

For devices the record is quantitative. We downloaded the FDA AI enabled medical device list in full: 1,614 authorizations current as of 4 September 2026, of which 1,230 (76.2%) are in radiology, 154 (9.5%) in cardiovascular, and 73 (4.5%) in neurology, rising from 18 authorizations in 2016 to 335 in 2025 and 181 in the first eight months of 2026{{c:fda_device_list}}. The January 2025 draft guidance on AI enabled device software functions asks for validation at "at least three geographically diverse US clinical sites" with two sided 95% confidence intervals{{c:fda_device_draft_guidance;fda_dhac_2024}}. The concentration in radiology is the number a health economist should notice: three quarters of authorized AI in American medicine operates in one specialty, so the cost effectiveness literature is being asked to generalize from a narrow deployment base.

The agency is also now a user. Elsa was deployed agency wide in June 2025 on a FedRAMP High environment, with the explicit statement that it does not train on data submitted by regulated industry{{c:fda_elsa}}. A regulator that uses these tools internally will develop views about their failure modes faster than one that only reviews them.

The European position is more prescriptive on one dimension. The European Medicines Agency reflection paper states that "the use of transparent models is preferred"{{c:ema_reflection_paper}}, a preference the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) echoes in requiring that where more explainable methods are potentially robust "those should be presented in the first instance, with supplementary use of less transparent approaches"{{c:nice_ecd11}}. The EU AI Act entered into force on 1 August 2024 with general purpose AI obligations applying from 2 August 2026{{c:eu_ai_act}}, and EFPIA has argued that AI used solely for medicines research and development is exempt under Recital 25 and Articles 2.6 and 2.8{{c:efpia_position}}. Industry association frameworks converge on similar content{{c:ifpma_ai_ethics;novartis_responsible_ai;roche_ai_ethics}}.

Payer side regulation has moved faster than either. The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services stated in February 2024 that "algorithms or artificial intelligence alone cannot be used as the basis to deny admission" in Medicare Advantage{{c:cms_hpms_2024}}, and California enacted Senate Bill 1120 requiring physician review of medical necessity determinations{{c:ca_sb1120}}. World Health Organization guidance on large multi-modal models notes hallucination rates of "at least 3 percent" and "as high as 27 percent"{{c:who_lmm_2024;who_ethics_2021}}. The asymmetry is worth stating plainly: using AI to deny care is now regulated in US statute, while using AI to generate the evidence that determines whether care is covered at all is governed by voluntary position statements.

### THE HTA RECORD

Table 2 sets the claims against the record.

NICE published its position statement on 15 August 2024, requiring demonstrable value, a preference for explainable methods, justification of assumptions using instruments such as PALISADE, and naming algorithmic bias, cybersecurity, and reduced human oversight as the principal risks{{c:nice_ecd11}}. Canada's Drug Agency followed in April 2025, drawing explicitly on NICE, permitting model generated search strategies, automated screening and extraction, meta-analysis code, and synthetic data and external control arms subject to transparent reporting, and framing the whole under augmentation rather than replacement{{c:cda_amc_position;10.51731/cjht.2024.1004;10.60584/general-methods_v8.0;10.57264/cer-2023-0154}}.

The workload argument for automation is real. Regulation (EU) 2021/2282 applies from 12 January 2025 for new oncology active substances and advanced therapy medicinal products, from 13 January 2028 for orphan products, and from 13 January 2030 for all others in scope{{c:eu_htar;ec_htar_implementation;ec_jca}}. The HTA Coordination Group estimated approximately 50 joint clinical assessments of medicines and approximately 5 of devices for 2026, with 12 ongoing and 14 countries involved as of December 2025{{c:ec_barbuto_presentation;htacg_awp_2026}}. A doubling of dossier volume with no increase in HEOR headcount is the strongest business case for automation available.

And yet disclosed use remains three documents out of 1,391, at NICE technology appraisals TA1154 for AI assisted study screening, TA1071 for AI assisted searches to identify treatment effect modifiers, and TA962 for a natural language processing approach, with no explicit reporting in any Canada's Drug Agency or IQWiG dossier{{c:ispor_audit_poster}}. A separate audit reported that seven agencies are adopting AI internally, with NICE recording 49 AI initiatives across 25 appraisals{{c:ispor_msr96}}. Agencies are adopting faster than submitters are disclosing. Conference activity confirms both volume and concentration: of 28,184 abstracts submitted to ISPOR and ISPE between January 2020 and December 2024, 1,153 addressed AI, with 89% of applications in literature review automation and 9% in economic modeling{{c:ispor_abstract_trends;ispor_sa14;op07_hta_scoping}}.

### THE INDUSTRY RECORD

For a pharmaceutical reader the question is not whether these tools work but whether the reported gains are the kind a budget can be built on. Table 2 and eTable 3 present the claims with provenance labelled.

Reported reductions in regulatory writing time span Pfizer at 40% faster first draft clinical study reports{{c:pfizer_ai}}, Merck at 180 hours to 80 hours per fully human reviewed first draft with errors reduced by 50%{{c:merck_genai}}, Novo Nordisk at more than ten weeks to ten minutes for clinical study documentation{{c:novo_nordisk_claude}}, Takeda at a projected halving of a 20 week tabulation process{{c:takeda_genai}}, and a Takeda and Weave Bio preprint at a 97% reduction, from about 100 hours to 3.7 hours for 18,870 pages across 61 source documents in one investigational new drug application{{c:weave_arxiv}}. Certara states the baseline these are measured against, about 120 hours of regulatory writing over four weeks and an average of 83 days from database lock to an approved clinical study report, without reporting a time saving for its own product{{c:certara_coauthor}}.

Every one of those figures is reported by the party deploying or selling the tool, and none has been independently replicated. The most methodologically explicit, the Weave and Takeda preprint, compares against a historical estimate with no concurrent control and an n of two. Elsewhere, Novartis reported site selection falling from four to six weeks to a two hour meeting for a 14,000 patient trial, and GSK reported approximately £8 million saved in late stage asthma studies{{c:reuters_drugmakers_ai;merck_google;lilly_nvidia;tempus_az;openai_moderna;novartis_sanofi_plai}}.

On the market access side the quantified evidence reduces to a single conference abstract. A retrieval augmented generation multi-agent pipeline ingesting 110 documents and generating sections per AMCP Format version 5.0 produced a draft reported as approximately 90% complete with approximately 80% less development time, while the authors noted repetition in all sections and subject matter experts who only somewhat agreed that responses were largely complete{{c:ispor_msr207;amcp_format}}. Vendor claims for payer landscape monitoring are unaudited{{c:norstella_ai;iqvia_market_access}}. For pricing, gross to net, contracting analytics, and international reference pricing we located no primary source with a measured, attributable efficiency or accuracy figure. That absence is itself a finding.

Against the efficiency claims stand the failures. DSP-1181, presented as the first AI designed drug when it entered the clinic in January 2020, was abandoned in January 2022 for failing to meet study criteria, and EXS-21546 was discontinued after modelling suggested a suitable therapeutic index would be difficult to reach, with Exscientia, BenevolentAI, and Recursion having accumulated a combined deficit above US$1.5 billion{{c:endpoints_ai_drugs;fierce_recursion}}. Independent analyses of AI discovered molecules report phase 1 success of 80% to 90% and phase 2 success of approximately 40%{{c:10.1016/j.drudis.2024.104009}}, against historical benchmarks of 13.8% overall probability of success from phase 1 to approval and 3.4% in oncology across 406,038 trial entries{{c:10.1093/biostatistics/kxx069;10.1093/biostatistics/kxy072}}. Counts of AI discovered candidates in trials range from 31 across eight companies{{c:10.1002/cpt.3542}} to 117 assets across 63 companies{{c:10.1200/jco.2026.44.16_suppl.11072}}, against a total pipeline of several thousand. Phase 2 is where efficacy is tested, and phase 2 success for AI discovered molecules sits at the historical average, judged against a cost per approved drug of US$2,558 million capitalized pre-approval in 2013 dollars{{c:10.1016/j.jhealeco.2016.01.012}} or a median of US$985.3 million (95% CI 683.6 to 1,228.9) in 2018 dollars{{c:10.1001/jama.2020.1166;10.1001/jama.2022.14317;10.1001/jama.2022.14733}}.

### FAILURE MODES

Four failure modes are documented well enough to be expected rather than exceptional.

Bias in deployed algorithms. Obermeyer and colleagues showed that a commercial risk algorithm applied to approximately 200 million Americans, by using cost as a proxy for need, identified 17.7% of Black patients for extra help where correcting the target would have identified 46.5%{{c:10.1126/science.aax2342}}. The mechanism was label choice, not model class, which is why it is a HEOR problem: the outcome variable was a health economics variable.

Weak external validity. The Epic sepsis model, evaluated externally across 38,455 hospitalizations, achieved area under the curve of 0.63 (95% CI 0.62 to 0.64) against a vendor reported range of 0.76 to 0.83{{c:10.1001/jamainternmed.2021.2626}}. High risk of bias is the norm rather than the exception here{{c:10.1136/bmj.m1328;10.1136/bmj.n2281}}.

Post deployment decay. Rahmani and colleagues documented data drift across 112,972 encounters{{c:10.1101/2022.06.06.22276062}}, and Kopanitsa found across four deployed clinical AI systems that calibration degraded earlier and more consistently than discrimination{{c:10.1371/journal.pdig.0001534}}. For HEOR this is the crux, because a cost effectiveness result depends on calibration, not on ranking. A model that still discriminates well and no longer calibrates will keep passing the metric that is monitored and stop supporting the estimate that is used.

Human factors. Jabbour and colleagues showed in a randomized vignette study of 457 clinicians that a standard AI raised diagnostic accuracy from a baseline of 73.0% by 2.9 percentage points, while a systematically biased AI lowered it by 11.3 points, with explanations providing no significant protection{{c:10.1001/jama.2023.22295}}. Dratsch and colleagues found experienced radiologists falling from 82.3% to 45.5% accuracy when shown an incorrect AI suggestion{{c:10.1148/radiol.222176;10.1093/jamia/ocad118;10.2196/58666}}, and Budzyń and colleagues reported adenoma detection in non-AI-assisted colonoscopies falling by 6.0 percentage points after AI exposure, from 28.4% to 22.4% (95% CI 10.5 to 1.6 lower; p = 0.0089){{c:10.1016/s2468-1253(25)00133-5;10.1016/s2468-1253(25)00324-3}}. Goh and colleagues randomized 50 physicians and found diagnostic reasoning scores of 76% with a large language model against 74% without, an adjusted difference of 2 percentage points (95% CI 4 lower to 8 higher; p = 0.60), while the model alone scored 16 points higher than physicians (95% CI 2 to 30; p = 0.03){{c:10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.40969}}. The model beat the physician and beat the physician using the model. Any workflow that assumes a human reviewer will catch the model's errors is assuming away the best measured finding in this literature.

Fabrication is the failure mode most specific to evidence work. Chelli and colleagues assessed 471 references and found hallucination rates of 39.6% for GPT-3.5, 28.6% for GPT-4, and 91.4% for Bard{{c:10.2196/53164}}, and Walters and Wilder examined 636 citations and found 55% fabricated by GPT-3.5 and 18% by GPT-4{{c:10.1038/s41598-023-41032-5}}. This has already reached consequential documents: a US federal health policy report cited studies that do not appear to exist, with a listed first author confirming she had never written the paper attributed to her{{c:science_maha}}, a filed expert declaration in federal court contained a citation to a nonexistent statistics article{{c:reuters_anthropic_expert;wsba_ledouxx}}, and one journal retracted 129 papers in the first six weeks of 2025{{c:retraction_watch_springer}}. No equivalent incident has yet been documented inside a drug regulatory or HTA submission. That is the state of the record, not a reassurance.

### THE ECONOMIC EVALUATION OF AI ITSELF

HEOR is being asked to evaluate AI and has barely done so. El Arab and colleagues found 19 economic evaluations of AI in health care published from 2020 through 2024, with CHEERS scores between 21 and 27 of 28{{c:10.1038/s41746-025-01722-y}}. Nineteen evaluations against 1,614 authorized AI enabled devices{{c:fda_device_list}} is the field's most direct self indictment. eTable 1 lists them.

The evaluations that exist are instructive. Mital and Nguyen estimated an incremental cost effectiveness ratio of US$23,755 per quality adjusted life year for AI versus polygenic risk scores in breast cancer risk stratification, with an AI cost threshold of US$318 per mammogram{{c:10.1186/s12885-022-09613-1;10.1038/s41591-021-01599-w}}. Against this, Vargas-Palacios and colleagues modelled AI in the UK breast screening programme and found a scenario in which no price per scan made the technology cost effective{{c:10.1038/s41467-023-41754-0}}. That is the result to hold onto: for a diagnostic AI, cost effectiveness is frequently determined by the downstream care pathway rather than by the price of the algorithm, so a procurement negotiated on price per scan is negotiating the wrong parameter{{c:10.1016/j.jval.2021.08.015;10.1186/s12913-022-07655-6;10.1016/j.ijtb.2025.11.019}}.

The regulatory record on external controls is more informative than the synthetic data literature acknowledges. Jahanshahi and colleagues identified 45 FDA approvals between 2000 and 2019 that accepted external control data, 36 (80%) in rare disease{{c:10.1007/s43441-021-00302-y;10.1080/10543406.2021.2011901}}, and in its qualification opinion on prognostic covariate adjustment the EMA Committee for Medicinal Products for Human Use stated that excluding dropout data "would not be acceptable for regulatory purposes"{{c:ema_procova}}. The model may be qualified, and the analysis population still has to be complete.

### TRANSPARENCY, DISCLOSURE, AND WORKFORCE

The baseline is poor before AI is introduced. In a random sample of 200 health economic evaluations, 10 (5%) mentioned access to raw data and analytic code{{c:10.1016/j.jclinepi.2023.10.024}}, and in healthcare discrete event simulation 47 of 564 studies (8.3%) cited an openly available model{{c:10.1080/17477778.2023.2260772;10.1016/j.jval.2025.01.019;10.1371/journal.pone.0275380;10.1136/bmj.k400}}. Replication attempts are sobering: of five economic analyses re-implemented, two were fully replicated and two were unequivocally not replicable{{c:10.1007/s40273-017-0553-x}}, and four obesity models rebuilt from 87 publications showed cost utility ratio deviations from 3.0% lower to 17.9% higher{{c:10.1007/s40273-021-01008-7}}.

Disclosure of AI use runs far below reported use. Journals and societies have converged on rules that prohibit AI authorship and require disclosure of model name, version, and dates{{c:icmje_ai;cope_ai;nejm_ai_policies;nejm_ai_about;jama_ifora;health_affairs_requirements;elsevier_genai_policy;nature_ai_policy;ispor_ai_policy;vih_guide_authors}}. eTable 4 tabulates them. Against those rules, 0.6% of Lancet submissions in February 2024 declared ChatGPT use and 1.8% declared AI as a research tool{{c:lancet_disclosure_editorial}}, while surveys report use between 25% and 81%, including more than half of about 1,600 academics across 111 countries having used AI in peer review and 81% of 1,692 physicians aware of or using AI in 2026 against 38% in 2023{{c:10.1038/d41586-025-01463-8;10.1038/d41586-025-04066-5;ama_sentiment;ama_press_2026;mckinsey_genai_health;himss_medscape}}. eTable 5 gives the survey estimates.

Within HEOR the workforce is not yet equipped. Of 133 HEOR, real-world data, and medical affairs professionals surveyed in April 2026, 61% rated their AI proficiency as basic or very limited and 4% as expert{{c:polygon_survey}}. Among 100 HEOR leaders at 72 companies, 69% personally use AI, 29% expect AI to replace some HEOR staff within two years, and 59% report that their team does not use AI for any listed HEOR workflow{{c:tufts_cevr_poster}}. Medical affairs reports higher confidence than capability, with 49% still using full manual expert review of every AI generated output{{c:maps_workflows;zs_medical_affairs}}. Competency frameworks are appearing but none is HEOR specific{{c:10.2196/91116;10.3389/fdgth.2025.1584883;10.1002/hsr2.72066;10.4300/jgme-d-25-00009.1;10.1080/0142159x.2024.2314198;10.1016/j.jval.2020.04.1834}}, while capital has already moved: AI enabled startups took 54% of US digital health funding in 2025 against 37% in 2024{{c:rock_health_2025;rock_health_2024;white_case_life_sciences;mckinsey_ai_budgets;grand_view_ai_health}}.

## Discussion

The governance finding is the sharpest contrast in this series. Adoption claims run at 40% to 97% time savings in pharmaceutical regulatory writing and approximately 80% in payer dossier drafting, while disclosed use in HTA submissions is 3 of 1,391 documents (0.2%) and declared use in journal submissions is 0.6%. There are only three explanations. Either the tools are not actually used in submission grade work, which the industry evidence makes implausible. Or they are used and not disclosed, which the survey evidence makes likely. Or the disclosure requirement is understood to apply to authorship rather than to method, so a systematic review screened by a large language model is not thought to require declaration in the way a manuscript drafted by one would be. The third explanation carries most of the weight, and it is fixable, because it is a definitional failure rather than a compliance failure. Current instruments ask whether AI wrote the text. Reviewers need to know whether AI selected the evidence, defined the cohort, extracted the parameter, or generated the code.

### WHAT ACEMOGLU'S CRITIQUE MEANS FOR THIS FIELD

Daron Acemoglu, awarded the 2024 Sveriges Riksbank Prize in Economic Sciences in Memory of Alfred Nobel with Simon Johnson and James Robinson{{c:nobel_econ_2024}}, estimates total factor productivity gains no greater than 0.66% over ten years, roughly 0.064% annually, with gross domestic product effects near 1%. The estimate derives from a task based decomposition rather than from pessimism about model capability{{c:10.1093/epolic/eiae042;10.3386/w32487}}. His central mechanism is the distinction between easy and hard learning tasks. A task is easy to learn when outcomes are observed, labels are abundant, and feedback is fast, and hard to learn when the correct answer is not observed. His illustration is clinical: diagnosing a persistent cough is hard because "there is no large, well-curated data set of successful diagnoses and cures."

Health economics is the limiting case of a hard learning task, for structural rather than technological reasons. The quantity of interest is a counterfactual, so no data set of correct incremental cost effectiveness ratios exists, will exist, or could exist. This is why the empirical pattern reported in Part 1 is exactly what his framework predicts: the gains disappear precisely where the label disappears.

His second mechanism is task share. Even large improvements on a small share of total task time yield small aggregate gains{{c:10.1257/jep.33.2.3;10.1257/aer.20160696}}. Applied to HEOR, the clerical layer is where the 40% to 97% figures live, and it is a minority of the total work and the least remunerated portion of it. The judgment layer, choosing the comparator, specifying the model structure, defending the extrapolation, negotiating the price, is unaffected. Under this arithmetic an 80% reduction in a task that is 20% of the total yields a 16% aggregate gain in the best case, before accounting for the review burden the automation creates.

The counterargument deserves fair statement, because it is empirically supported. Noy and Zhang found writing time down 40% and quality up 18% across 453 professionals with the largest gains for weaker performers{{c:10.1126/science.adh2586}}, Brynjolfsson and colleagues found 14% to 15% more resolutions per hour across 5,179 support agents with 34% gains for novices{{c:10.1093/qje/qjae044;10.3386/w31161}}, and Peng and colleagues found programmers 55.8% faster{{c:10.48550/arxiv.2302.06590}}. Three features are uncomfortable for HEOR. The gains concentrate in the least experienced, which compresses the junior training pathway through which HEOR expertise has been built. They are largest where output is verifiable, which HEOR output is not. And the direction reverses outside the frontier of capability: in a randomized study of 758 consultants, those using AI inside that frontier completed 12.2% more tasks 25.1% faster, while on a task outside it accuracy fell from 84.5% to 70.6% with AI and to 60.0% with AI plus an overview of its limits{{c:10.1287/orsc.2025.21838}}. The overview made it worse. HEOR's hardest tasks sit outside the frontier, and its practitioners cannot yet reliably tell which side a given task is on, since 61% rate their proficiency as basic or very limited{{c:polygon_survey}}.

What follows is not that AI will not matter. It is that the mechanism by which it matters will be cost structure rather than evidence quality, and that the two are separable. The realistic ten year expectation is a HEOR function producing more dossiers per analyst at similar quality, with a compressed junior tier, a widened tail of low quality submissions from parties who could not previously afford to submit at all, and a corresponding increase in the burden on reviewers. Reviewers, not producers, absorb the externality. That is the case for the reporting standard below, and it is an economic case rather than an ethical one.

### TRACE-HEOR: A REPORTING STANDARD FOR AI ASSISTED HEOR EVIDENCE

Existing instruments cover the parts of this problem that resemble clinical prediction or clinical trials: TRIPOD+AI and PROBAST+AI for prediction model reporting and bias{{c:10.1136/bmj-2023-078378;10.1136/bmj-2024-082505}}, CONSORT-AI and SPIRIT-AI for trials of AI interventions{{c:10.1038/s41591-020-1034-x;10.1038/s41591-020-1037-7}}, DECIDE-AI for early live clinical evaluation{{c:10.1038/s41591-022-01772-9}}, STARD-AI for diagnostic accuracy{{c:10.1136/bmjopen-2020-047709}}, TRIPOD-LLM for large language model studies{{c:10.1038/s41591-024-03425-5}}, CANGARU for disclosure in scholarly writing{{c:10.48550/arxiv.2307.08974}}, CHEERS 2022 for economic evaluation reporting{{c:10.1016/j.jval.2021.11.1351}}, PALISADE for real-world data study credibility{{c:10.1016/j.jval.2022.03.022}}, and ELEVATE-GenAI with the ISPOR taxonomy for the HEOR specific work{{c:10.1016/j.jval.2025.06.018;10.1016/j.jval.2025.04.2167;10.1016/j.jval.2024.10.3846}}. None answers the question an HTA reviewer actually has, which is whether the reimbursement recommendation would change if the AI component were removed.

We propose TRACE-HEOR, for Transparent Reporting of Artificial intelligence Components in health Economics and Outcomes Research: ten items designed as an extension to CHEERS 2022 and PALISADE rather than as a replacement, and stated so that each is verifiable by a reviewer with access only to the submission. Table 3 presents them in full with the evidence supporting each.

1. Declare the function, not the tool. State, for each AI component, which of the following it performed: evidence identification or screening, data extraction, cohort or exposure definition, outcome or endpoint derivation, covariate selection, effect estimation, parameter derivation, model code generation, synthetic data or external control generation, or narrative drafting. Name the model, version, vendor, and dates of use, as JAMA already requires{{c:jama_ifora}}.
2. Specify the context of use and the model risk, using the FDA's two factor definition of model influence and decision consequence{{c:fda_draft_ai_guidance}}, and state which decision the model influences.
3. Report calibration, not only discrimination, in the deployment population and time period. Where an estimate depends on absolute risk or absolute cost, discrimination alone is insufficient{{c:10.1371/journal.pdig.0001534}}.
4. Report the human reference standard and its own reliability. Where AI output is validated against human abstraction, report inter-abstractor agreement for the reference{{c:10.1007/s12325-021-01659-0;10.1200/cci-25-00215}}.
5. Report performance for the variables the analysis depends on, disaggregated. Aggregate F1 conceals the pattern in which stage extraction reaches 0.988 precision while histology linkage reaches 0.79 recall{{c:10.1200/cci.23.00099}} and T stage reaches F1 0.89 while M stage reaches 0.24{{c:10.1200/cci.21.00065}}.
6. Report a decision reversal analysis. Re-estimate the primary result with the AI component replaced by the pre-AI alternative, or by a plausible worst case, and state whether the incremental cost effectiveness ratio crosses the relevant threshold or whether the recommendation changes. This item exists in no current instrument and is the one reviewers need most.
7. Report bias and subgroup performance for the populations the decision affects, and state the demographic composition of any training or synthetic population{{c:10.1126/science.aax2342;10.1002/trc2.70181}}.
8. For synthetic data or model based external controls, report generator family, fidelity against the real target quantity rather than against summary statistics, and a privacy evaluation. Generator choice alone moved recovery of the true median progression free survival from 6.5% to 98.1% of replicates{{c:10.2196/55118;10.1038/s41746-024-01359-3}}.
9. Report the human oversight actually performed, quantitatively: what fraction of AI output was reviewed, by whom, with what qualifications, and what the disagreement rate was{{c:maps_workflows;10.1001/jama.2023.22295;10.1148/radiol.222176;10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.40969}}.
10. Deposit prompts, code, model versions, seeds, and intermediate outputs in a citable archive at submission. Five percent of health economic evaluations currently mention code availability{{c:10.1016/j.jclinepi.2023.10.024}}, and an AI assisted analysis is not auditable without the prompts.

Items 1, 2, 6, 9, and 10 are the TRACE-HEOR minimum set, and a submission reporting only those five is materially more auditable than any submission we located. If an agency were to adopt only one, it should be item 6, because decision reversal is the only item that converts a technical property of a model into the currency a reimbursement committee reasons in.

### A RESEARCH AGENDA

Five priorities follow, ordered by the ratio of decision relevance to current investment.

First, benchmark AI derived endpoints against regulatory grade human abstraction in a multi-sponsor, multi-vendor study with a pre-registered protocol, and publish the discordant cases. Current validation is vendor authored{{c:10.1200/cci-25-00215;10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2025.0128;10.2196/64697}}, which is a conflict rather than a failing, and the remedy is independent replication.

Second, obtain a regulatory precedent. Sponsors should seek scientific advice or a qualification opinion on an AI derived endpoint, and agencies should publish the reasoning when they give it, as the EMA did on prognostic covariate adjustment{{c:ema_procova}}. One published opinion would be worth more to this field than fifty conference abstracts.

Third, establish an empirical decision reversal literature. Take completed cost effectiveness analyses and HTA submissions, substitute AI assisted components for the original ones, and measure how often the recommendation changes. The replication literature indicates the base rate of non-replication is already high before AI is introduced{{c:10.1007/s40273-017-0553-x;10.1007/s40273-021-01008-7}}, which makes the marginal question both answerable and urgent.

Fourth, run randomized trials of AI assisted HEOR workflows with quality, not speed, as the primary endpoint. Every existing industry efficiency figure is single arm and self reported, and the designs already exist in adjacent fields{{c:10.1287/orsc.2025.21838;10.1126/science.adh2586;10.1016/j.jval.2025.08.002}}.

Fifth, evaluate AI itself economically at a scale proportionate to its deployment, and build HEOR specific competency and audit capacity. Nineteen published economic evaluations against 1,614 authorized devices is a ratio of about one to eighty five{{c:10.1038/s41746-025-01722-y;fda_device_list}}, the finding that no price per scan made an AI cost effective in a national screening programme should be reproduced or refuted{{c:10.1038/s41467-023-41754-0;10.1016/j.jval.2021.08.015}}, and reviewer side capacity is the binding constraint because joint clinical assessment volume rises on a fixed schedule{{c:eu_htar;htacg_awp_2026;polygon_survey}}.

### LIMITATIONS

The regulatory, HTA, and industry record assembled here is a narrative review rather than a systematic one, and negative results are less visible in it: a sponsor that abandoned an AI assisted approach has no reason to publish that fact. Several central quantities rest on conference abstracts and company communications, which we label rather than exclude because they are the only available source. The disclosure denominators are audits of published documents, so they measure disclosure and not use. TRACE-HEOR is a proposal derived from documented failure modes rather than a consensus instrument; it has not been through a Delphi process, and it should be treated as a starting draft for one.

## Conclusions

Regulators have written more about artificial intelligence in evidence generation than sponsors have disclosed using it. The FDA has authorized 1,614 AI enabled devices and received more than 500 drug and biologic submissions containing AI components, while its credibility framework remains a draft that excludes discovery and operational uses. Industry reports time savings of 40% to 97% in regulatory writing, none independently replicated. Three of 1,391 health technology assessment documents disclose an AI component.

The gap is not primarily a compliance problem. Disclosure instruments were built to establish who wrote a document, and the operations that matter in this field are the ones that select the evidence, define the cohort, derive the endpoint, and generate the code. Under the task based productivity arithmetic that Acemoglu applies to the wider economy, the tasks AI does well in health economics are the minority of the work and the least remunerated part of it, while the burden of checking the output falls on reviewers who did not choose the tool. TRACE-HEOR states ten items that would make an AI assisted submission auditable, and one of them, a decision reversal analysis, would tell a reimbursement committee the only thing it needs to know.
